# Guide to 10× Your Focus & Memory

- **Format:** *Figuring Out* podcast, hosted by Raj Shamani, featuring cognitive neuroscientist Dr Sahar Yousef.
- **Published:** 8 September 2026 · **Duration:** about 2 hours 55 minutes.
- **Supporting reference used for this note:** [public transcript](https://www.usetranscribe.io/yt/4Vz6L8B73i4/train-your-brain-focus) and the [episode chapters](https://podfollow.com/raj-shamanis-figuring-out/episode/0698217a7fc411258c7217157783986f15491b01/view).

> This is a practical summary of the conversation, not a transcript or medical advice. Some points are the guest's interpretations and recommendations; treat them as ideas to test, and seek qualified help for persistent sleep, mental-health, or burnout concerns.

## Core idea

Focus, memory, motivation, and recovery are trainable. The video argues that better performance does not come from working continuously or consuming more information; it comes from working with attention, energy, habits, and the environment.

## Learning from the video

### 1. Treat attention as a limited resource

- The brain pays a time and energy cost each time it switches tasks. The demonstration at the beginning shows that alternating between two simple tasks takes far longer and produces more mistakes than completing them one at a time.
- “Multitasking” is usually rapid task switching. You have one pool of attention, so dividing it lowers the quality of each task.
- **Takeaway:** For important work, choose one task, remove competing tasks, and finish a meaningful block before switching.

### 2. Design the environment before relying on willpower

- A visible phone can pull attention even when it is not being used. Notifications, open tabs, messages, and nearby devices continually invite the brain to reorient.
- Put the phone out of sight or in another room for deep work and meaningful conversations. Silence nonessential notifications and close unrelated tabs.
- **Takeaway:** Make focus the default setup, rather than repeatedly fighting distraction.

### 3. Memory starts with attention

- You cannot reliably remember what you never properly attended to. Attention is presented as the gateway through which information reaches memory.
- Short, rapidly changing content can create a feeling of being informed without creating strong recall or learning.
- **Takeaway:** When studying, read or watch one substantial item, take notes in your own words, and recall the key ideas without looking.

### 4. Use identity-based beliefs, not only goals

- The guest frames the default mode network (DMN) as the brain’s internal storyteller: the stories about identity, possibility, and the future affect what we notice and pursue.
- Replace an unhelpful story with one connected to values, evidence, benefits, and a repeatable action. Review it regularly rather than waiting to “feel motivated.”
- **Simple template:** *I am the kind of person who [desired behaviour], because [value/evidence], which helps me [benefit].*
- **Example:** *I protect my sleep because it improves my health and my performance; I begin winding down at 11:00 pm.*

### 5. Turn vague intentions into specific if–then plans

- “I should sleep earlier” or “I should focus more” is too vague. Describe the cue, place, time, and first action.
- The video calls this an implementation intention or situation–action plan.
- **Template:** *When [clear cue] happens, I will [small, visible action] at [place/time].*
- **Example:** *When my 11:00 pm alarm rings, I put my phone outside the bedroom, start my wind-down playlist, and make herbal tea.*

### 6. Break unwanted habits at the feeling, not only at the behaviour

- Mindless scrolling is often an attempted escape from an unpleasant feeling—stress, boredom, overwhelm, or loneliness—not simply a desire for content.
- Insert a tiny cognitive pause before the habitual response. The episode’s example is 60 seconds of closing the eyes or breathing before deciding whether to scroll.
- The goal is not perfection: the pause creates a chance to choose differently and gradually strengthens a healthier route.
- **Takeaway:** Identify the trigger feeling, pause, then use a small replacement behaviour such as breathing, walking, water, or a brief stretch.

### 7. Protect boredom and low-stimulation time

- Insight often appears after stepping away from a loaded problem—not while adding more inputs. A walk, shower, cooking, or light exercise can introduce new cues while leaving room for thought.
- Constant feeds fill every silent moment and leave little room for reflection, creative association, or personal planning.
- **Takeaway:** Schedule short phone-free “thinking walks” or quiet periods. Do something light, not another immersive activity.

### 8. Prefer a deliberate content diet

- The video contrasts long-form learning and storytelling with continuous short-form novelty. Long-form content is more likely to support sustained attention, narrative understanding, and recall.
- Reduce content that makes you anxious or reactive, especially repeated news checking and rage-inducing feeds.
- Replace “doom scrolling” with “bloom scrolling”: intentionally follow creators and sources that teach, challenge, or support your real goals.
- **Takeaway:** Set a limit for short-form feeds; deliberately choose books, films, long podcasts, tutorials, and high-quality conversations.

### 9. Create phone boundaries around sleep and mornings

- The guest recommends putting the phone away 15–30 minutes before bed and delaying it for 15–30 minutes after waking.
- The point is to protect sleep and prevent notifications, news, and other people’s priorities from taking over the mind before you set your own direction.
- **Takeaway:** Use a separate alarm if possible. Start the day with water, movement, journaling, planning, or quiet time before checking messages.

### 10. Work with your chronotype, not an idealized schedule

- The video challenges the idea that everyone must join a “5 AM club.” The foundation is enough high-quality sleep; the useful work time varies by individual.
- Notice when your energy and clarity are highest. Protect that window for deep work; reserve lower-energy periods for administration, meetings, and routine tasks.
- **Takeaway:** Aim for at least seven hours of quality sleep and plan around your real peak, rather than copying someone else’s schedule.

### 11. Complete stress cycles to reduce burnout risk

- Stress and burnout are not identical. The episode describes stress as a normal activation cycle that needs a recovery phase; unbroken activation can become harmful over time.
- The proposed **3M framework** is:

  | Break | Suggested cadence | Purpose |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Micro break | A few minutes daily | Interrupt sustained activation and reset briefly. |
  | Meso break | 2–4 hours weekly | Create a longer period away from regular demands. |
  | Macro break | Half to a full day monthly | Recover more deeply and review the larger rhythm of work. |

- **Takeaway:** Do not wait for exhaustion. Put recovery blocks on the calendar just as you schedule focused work.

### 12. Replace digital coping with real connection and engaging activities

- The conversation links excessive scrolling with isolation and encourages real-world connection, play, hobbies, and emotional-regulation skills.
- A nine-week classroom intervention described in the episode combined lower short-form scrolling, social accountability, healthier coping strategies, in-person connection, and a hobby; the guest reports improved attention and lower loneliness, depression, and anxiety scores among participants.
- **Takeaway:** Do not only remove a habit. Replace it with a social, physical, creative, or challenging activity that makes life fuller.

## A practical focus protocol

1. Choose one important task for a 45–90 minute focus block.
2. Put the phone in another room; silence notifications and close unrelated tabs.
3. Write a precise next action before starting.
4. When the urge to check a feed appears, take a 60-second breathing pause first.
5. After the block, take a short walk, stretch, or quiet break—do not automatically switch to scrolling.
6. Review the day: what distracted you, what helped, and what one environmental change will you make tomorrow?

## Suggested weekly experiment

For one week, track four items:

- Deep-work minutes completed without the phone nearby.
- Short-form scrolling minutes.
- Sleep duration and the first/last phone use of the day.
- One real-world connection, hobby session, or phone-free walk.

At the end of the week, keep the changes that noticeably improve calm, concentration, sleep, or output.
